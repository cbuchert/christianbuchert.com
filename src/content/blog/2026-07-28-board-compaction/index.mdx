## TL;DR

> I want a compaction that packs *reasonably evenly* and *moves chips
> predictably*. Those two pull hard against each other: hole-free + move-local
> forces the lopsided corner staircase. A tempting escape is to fix the shape
> with the camera — but auto-panning the view on the player feels bad, so I've
> ruled it out, and the shape stays the packer's problem. This is the search for a
> packer that threads the needle.

## The setting

Paint Chips is a word-sorting puzzle played on a 2D grid. Every occupied cell
holds a stack — a chip — and I store the board as a sparse map from cell to
stack; empty cells simply aren't in the map. Cells are addressed as
`row * STRIDE + col` with a big fixed `STRIDE`, so a cell keeps its identity as
the occupied region grows, and numeric cell order is just reading order: top to
bottom, left to right.

When you pause, the board **compacts** — occupied cells slide together to close
gaps so the play area shrinks to fit. That slide is the compaction, and its
resting arrangement is the whole subject here.

What makes this hard is *what* triggers it. Two events, and both remove a stack
from an **interior** cell: a **merge** (two same-category stacks combine,
vacating the source cell) and a **category completion** (a finished stack leaves
the board). Real play is dominated by these mid-board removals. Hold onto that —
it's the crux of everything below.

## The problem: a lopsided board

The baseline is the obvious thing. Pack toward the top-left corner: slide every
row left against column 0, slide every column up against row 0, repeat until
nothing moves. Iterate that and it always converges to the same family of
shapes — a Young diagram, a staircase.

And the staircase has the problem built into its definition. In any top-left
staircase, row 0 is the widest row and column 0 is the tallest column — not by
accident, by construction. So over a game you grow a long top row and a long
left column, and the board reads as lopsided. It isn't a tuning bug you can dial
out; it's the mathematical signature of packing into a corner. Biasing which
axis packs first (wide-first vs tall-first) only swaps which of the two is
longer. Both maxima still live at the corner.

### Demo A — the staircase forms

Pull a few chips out and watch it settle. Two things to notice. It's
**hole-free** — every row is a solid run from the left, every column a solid run
from the top, no gaps that read as floating islands. And it's **local**: remove
a chip from the middle and only that row's tail shifts left, plus a one-cell
pull-up in the column above the gap. Nothing teleports. That locality is the
whole reason the baseline is livable — and, as we'll see, the whole reason it's
stuck being lopsided.

## The hard constraint: move-locality

Here's the rule any fix has to respect: chips can't slide **unpredictably.** If a
merge in one corner shoves a chip on the far side of the board across three
cells, the board stops feeling like a physical object and starts feeling like a
slot machine. This constraint has teeth — it already killed my first idea.

That idea was a **dynamic √ wrap**: take all `n` occupied stacks in reading order
and lay them into a `⌈√n⌉`-wide grid. You get a near-perfect square — exactly the
even shape I was after. On device it was a disaster: the whole board slid on
nearly every move. The root cause is that a chip's cell is derived from its rank
in **one global sequence** — remove a stack from the middle and everything after
it re-indexes by one, cascading down the board. And each time `n` crosses a √
boundary the row width changes and the entire thing re-wraps.

The corner-pack avoids this precisely because it packs each row and each column
**independently**, each anchored to a fixed edge. A removal stays local because
nothing downstream depends on a global count. In one measured mid-board merge,
corner-pack moved ~3 of 11 chips; √-wrap moved ~6.

The general version of the rule: locality doesn't require corner-packing, it
requires **per-line-rank positioning** — a chip's cell is a function of *which
line it's in and its rank within that line*, never its rank in a single global
order. Any total-order linearization cascades, and is out.

## The key insight: hole-free + local ⟹ the corner staircase

So I have two things I want from a compaction:

- **Hole-free:** every row a contiguous run from column 0, every column a
  contiguous run from row 0. No interior gaps that read as islands.
- **Move-local:** each line anchored to a fixed edge, so a change stays local.

Here's the uncomfortable part. *Those two properties, together, ARE the corner
staircase.* Contiguous-from-the-edge on both axes is the definition of the
staircase, and the staircase's defining feature is that its longest row and
longest column both sit at the anchor corner. The only thing you get to choose is
**which corner** — and that just relocates the long row and column to a different
edge. It never removes them.

That kills the tempting fixes one at a time:

- **Center it into a pyramid?** Centering a line means appending to it shifts
  chips at *both* ends — about half the line moves for one addition.
  Edge-anchoring is load-bearing; it's the entire reason corner-pack is local. So
  centering trades the shape straight back for the movement.
- **Just shorten the left column?** To do that you have to stop left-anchoring
  every row — which opens a hole *under* the rows you pull off the edge.

You cannot de-lopside at the packing layer without paying in either **holes** or
**unpredictable movement.** That's not a failure of imagination; it's the shape
of the problem. The next two demos are me paying each price and not liking the
bill.

### Demo C — brick's two failures

**Brick** was my attempt to break the "every row hugs the left edge" pattern:
even rows flush left, odd rows flush right. On the aggregate metrics it looked
like a win — lower elongation, less load on the origin edges. Then I put it on a
phone and it came apart two ways.

**Islands.** A short right-flushed row leaves an empty gap between it and the left
edge — an interior hole. I bought balance with holes, exactly as the theorem
warned.

**Teleporting.** Left-vs-right is chosen by row-index **parity.** A merge changes
a row's width, the staircase re-sorts widest-first, the row's index parity flips
— and the whole row jumps edge to edge in a single settle. The mean-chips-moved
number (~1.7× the baseline) makes that sound mild. A whole row snapping across
the board does not feel mild.

<CompactionDemo strategy="brick" caption="Brick. Watch islands appear, and whole rows teleport left↔right." />

### Demo D — the mirror's jitter

**Mirror-down** came from a nicer intuition: instead of the wide row hanging off
the top like it's glued to the ceiling, let it rest on the floor like a real
pile. I implemented it as a vertical mirror of the corner-packer's output — and
that's the trap.

Mirroring a corner-packer is **non-idempotent.** The up-pack sorts each column by
ascending row; the flip reverses that key, so when the loop recompacts an
already-settled board, the same cells come back with a *permuted* stack
assignment. Since the game recompacts on every pause, a resting board reshuffles
itself — chips moving with no move made. Try it: don't remove anything, just hit
Reset twice and watch the settle-jitter number.

(For the record: a **native** down-pack — a genuine bottom-anchored fixpoint, not
a mirror — doesn't jitter. But geometrically it's just the baseline upside down:
same elongation, same locality. Its only real effect is orientation, and whether
"resting on a floor" beats "hanging from a ceiling" is a perceptual question a
metric can't answer for me.)

## The camera non-answer

The agent I handed this to reached a clean conclusion: shape isn't the packer's
problem, it's the camera's. Keep the lopsided corner-pack, and after each settle
pan the viewport so the occupied **centroid** sits at the center of the view.
Zero chips move, so locality is untouched by construction, and the board simply
stops *looking* lopsided. (The subtlety worth keeping: center on the centroid of
occupied cells, not the bounding box — a staircase is top-left-heavy even inside
a perfectly centered box, so box-centering doesn't fix the perception and
centroid-centering does.)

It's a real result. It's just not one I'm willing to use — and here the *why*
matters, because it isn't a technical wall. The game can pan; that's not the
blocker. Moving the view out from under the player, on its own, without the
player asking for it, feels bad. It's the kind of thing that makes an interface
feel like it has a mind of its own, and it trades a shape complaint for a worse
one. So I'm treating a self-panning camera as off the table by choice, and the
shape stays the packer's problem.

## The open problem: a packer that threads the needle

Which leaves me here. The theorem says even shape, hole-free, and move-local
can't all three hold at once, and camera-recenter — the fourth escape — is off
the table. So a better packer has to give up **one** of the three, but in a
*controlled* way, not the flailing way brick and √-wrap gave it up.

What I'm poking at:

- **Bounded holes.** Allow some interior gaps, but keep them small and
  predictable. A hole that's always in the same place is furniture; a hole that
  teleports is a bug. Brick failed because its holes moved.
- **Bounded movement.** Cap the chips a single event can move and accept a
  slightly worse shape, instead of √-wrap's unbounded cascade.
- **A softer anchor.** A per-line rank rule that's less rigidly corner-locked but
  still a pure function of (line, rank-in-line), so it stays local while spreading
  the mass more evenly.

No winner yet. What a winner has to satisfy: elongation near 1 (even-ish), low
chips-moved (local), a *predictable direction* of movement, and no whole-row
teleports. If you've solved something like this, I'd genuinely like to hear it.

## How to evaluate this (the methodology)

One thing I'd do differently from the start: rank these on **data** before ever
touching a phone. Every strategy here is a pure function on the board map, so the
harness is just more pure functions — no UI required. Start from a full grid,
repeatedly remove a random interior stack (a stand-in for a merge or completion),
recompact, sample the metrics, down to a small floor.

Two details that matter more than they look. Use a **seeded PRNG** so runs
reproduce across machines. And pick the removal victim by rank in a **sorted
stack-id list**, so every strategy faces the *identical* removal stream — that
isolates layout as the only variable.

Four metrics, all in [`./demos/compaction.ts`](https://github.com/cbuchert/christianbuchert.com):

- **`countChipsMoved`** — the locality number, and the one I trust most. Count the
  stacks present in both the before and after boards whose cell changed. (A
  removed stack is the *event*, not a slide, so it doesn't count.)
- **`elongation`** — lopsidedness. `(maxRowRun + maxColRun) / (2·⌈√n⌉)`. 1.0 is a
  perfect square; higher is more staircase.
- **`originEdgeLoad`** — the literal complaint. How much of the mass sits on
  row 0 and column 0. 1.0 means both origin edges span the whole block.
- **settle-jitter** — recompact an already-settled board and count what moved.
  Must be **0.** This is the hard gate that disqualified the mirror.

Run the seeded replay (grid side 6, 5 seeds, removals down to 2 stacks) and the
trade falls right out — `moved` shown mean / p90 / max:

| strategy | moved | elong (mean/max) | originLoad | jitter | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline (corner) | 2.2 / 5 / 9 | 1.09 / 1.50 | 1.00 | 0 | local + hole-free, but lopsided — the status quo |
| √-wrap | 9.7 / 22 / 34 | 0.95 / 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 | square, but cascades — not local |
| brick | 3.8 / 9 / 17 | 0.95 / 1.25 | 0.81 | 0 | better shape on paper; islands + teleport on device |
| mirror-down | 17.2 / 31 / 34 | 1.08 / 1.33 | 0.77 | **34** | disqualified — settle-jitter |
| native-down | 2.7 / 5 / 14 | 1.08 / 1.38 | 0.78 | 0 | just the baseline mirrored — orientation only |

And three things the exercise taught me about measuring itself:

1. **Measure the representative event, not the convenient one.** The √-wrap
   prototype passed a "just append at the tail" test and then died on device —
   because tail-append is the *one* operation √-wrap is stable under, and real
   play is mid-sequence removals, the worst case. A misleading test is worse than
   no test.
2. **Aggregates hide qualitative pain.** Brick's ~1.7× mean-moved sounds fine. A
   whole-row teleport is not fine. The average ate the thing that actually
   mattered — which is why the table above doesn't rank brick as a winner despite
   its numbers.
3. **Metrics have blind spots.** Elongation and origin-load never once caught
   brick's interior holes — only the phone did. If you want to catch that on
   paper, add a fill-density metric (occupied / bounding-box area).
